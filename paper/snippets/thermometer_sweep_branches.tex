\paragraph{Encoding-width hypothesis confirmed.}
To test whether the address-tap saturation is bounded by the 8-bit
thermometer encoding rather than the address-tap width itself, we
re-trained the 96n $\times$ 32b and 500n $\times$ 34b peak architectures
at 16, 32, and 64-bit thermometer encodings on the full 46M dataset
(2 seeds per cell). Both architectures show meaningful F1 lift at
wider encodings (Table~\ref{tab:thermo-sweep}): the 96n $\times$ 32b
configuration improves from F1\,=\,A1\_F1\_8B\% (8-bit baseline) to
A1\_F1\_BEST\% at A1\_BEST\_BITS-bit, a +A1\_LIFT-point gain. The 500n
$\times$ 34b configuration shows a similar +A2\_LIFT-point gain at
A2\_BEST\_BITS-bit. \textbf{The 32-bit address-tap saturation observed
in Table~\ref{tab:ciot-46m} was therefore an encoding-layer ceiling,
not an address-tap-layer ceiling}: with $\log_2(\text{patterns})$
effective bits per feature scaling from 3.17 (8-bit) to 4.09 (16-bit)
to 6.02 (64-bit), wider thermometer encoding genuinely unlocks more
discriminative information at the input layer.

\paragraph{Encoding-width hypothesis ruled out.}
To test whether the address-tap saturation is bounded by the 8-bit
thermometer encoding rather than the address-tap width itself, we
re-trained the 96n $\times$ 32b and 500n $\times$ 34b peak architectures
at 16, 32, and 64-bit thermometer encodings on the full 46M dataset
(2 seeds per cell). Neither architecture shows meaningful F1
movement: the 96n $\times$ 32b stays within $\pm$A1\_DELTApp of the
8-bit baseline (B1\_F1\_8B\% $\to$ B1\_F1\_RANGE\%), and the 500n
$\times$ 34b similarly stays within $\pm$A2\_DELTApp (B2\_F1\_8B\%
$\to$ B2\_F1\_RANGE\%) (Table~\ref{tab:thermo-sweep}).
\textbf{The 32-bit address-tap is the genuine discrimination ceiling
on this dataset}, irrespective of input encoding richness ---
consistent with the hypothesis that thermometer-encoding bit
correlation already saturates the address-tap's effective sampling
even at 8-bit width.

\paragraph{Wider thermometer dilutes the signal.}
Re-training the 96n $\times$ 32b and 500n $\times$ 34b peak
architectures at 16, 32, and 64-bit thermometer encodings (2 seeds
per cell) shows F1 \emph{degradation} relative to the 8-bit baseline
on both architectures: the 96n $\times$ 32b drops by C1\_DROPpp at
64-bit thermometer (C1\_F1\_8B\% $\to$ C1\_F1\_64B\%), and the 500n
$\times$ 34b drops by C2\_DROPpp similarly (Table~\ref{tab:thermo-sweep}).
This is the \emph{dilution effect}: at 64-bit thermometer the
input vector grows to 1280 bits, of which a 32-bit address tap
samples only 2.5\% per neuron. The discriminative information per
neuron drops faster than the per-feature information rises.
The 8-bit thermometer is therefore the local optimum for this
combination of feature-set size and address-tap width on
CIC-IoT-2023, replicating the existing finding from the 1.3M
subsample sweep~\cite{paper-discussion}.

\paragraph{Architecture-dependent encoding sensitivity.}
The two peak architectures behave differently across thermometer
widths: the 96n $\times$ 32b shows D1\_VERDICT (D1\_F1\_8B\% $\to$
D1\_F1\_BEST\% at D1\_BEST\_BITS-bit, D1\_LIFT-point change), while
the 500n $\times$ 34b shows D2\_VERDICT (D2\_F1\_8B\% $\to$
D2\_F1\_BEST\% at D2\_BEST\_BITS-bit, D2\_LIFT-point change)
(Table~\ref{tab:thermo-sweep}). The contrasting responses suggest
that the saturation interacts with the address-tap-to-input ratio:
the smaller-neuron architecture has comparatively more capacity to
absorb wider input encodings, while the larger architecture is
already information-saturated at the 8-bit baseline.
